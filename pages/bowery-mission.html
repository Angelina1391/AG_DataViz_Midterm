227 Bowery

No. 227 Bowery is significant for its 103-year history as the home of the Bowery Mission, a religious-based organization that has fed, housed and cared for countless homeless individuals on the Lower East Side for more than 130 years. This building is also an important reminder of the Bowery’s history during the 19th century containing numerous homeless shelters run by missions and cheap lodging houses.

Bowery Mission

The Bowery History

The Bowery is the oldest thoroughfare on Manhattan Island and is part of the Wickquasgeck Road, made by the Indigenous peoples living in Manhattan before European settlement. This road was widened during Dutch colonization and linked the city of New Amsterdam with a group of “bouweries” or farms. These farms were established by the Dutch West India Company to supply the colony of New Amsterdam. After the British took control, this “Bowry Lane” became part of the Post Road, linking New York City and Boston. It was officially named “The Bowery” in 1813.

between 1643 and 1651, former slaves of the Dutch West India Company received ground briefs for land between the public wagon road (The Bowery) and the Fresh Water (Collect) and vicinity, creating one of the first African-American neighborhoods in Manhattan. These briefs were offered strategically; to older recipients so that the company could avoid expenses to caring for them. The settlement was also placed as a buffer between the Indigenous People and the Dutch colony of New Amsterdam. Freedom was conditional and emancipated slaves owed an annual tribute of produce or labor to the company when needed. However, in 1712, under British rule a concerted effort began to limit the growing black population by stripping them of their privileges and requiring the forfeit of their lands to the Crown.

The vulnerability of these scattered farms to Native American attacks prompted an order in 1660 that settlers gather in towns “after the English fashion”, and Bowery Village was established along the Bowery Lane. The area developed rapidly following the turn of the 19th century, and by the 1830s had become a bustling neighborhood comprised of brick and brick-fronted Federal style row houses. By the mid-19th century, as wealthier citizens moved uptown, the Bowery became more commercial in character.

After the Civil War, the Bowery became known for its cheap amusements. Music halls, dramatic theaters, dive bars, pawnbrokers, medicine shows, and museums exhibiting sword swallowers, exotic animals, and scantily-clad women lined the streets. The Bowery also had a reputation for religion, with the first mission in the East Village, the Gospel Rescue Mission, being founded in 1872. The Bowery Mission, established in 1879, was the second mission on the Bowery, and the third in the nation, becoming a leading provider of charity and evangelism.

19th-20th Century

In the early 19th century, the Bowery functioned as “the grand avenue of the respectable lower classes”, where Federal-era houses were converted to saloons and boarding houses, standing in great contrast to grand architectural showpieces constructed by the neighborhood’s cultural and financial institutions. Boarding houses were common and widespread and were not stigmatized as they would come to be in later years. Young single workers arriving in New York sought housing in reputable boarding houses, often in lower wards near the trades, industries, and businesses in which they worked. The entertainment culture of the Bowery arose to

serve these working-class individuals lodging on or around the Bowery. As the entertainment district on the Bowery degenerated over the course of the second half of the 19th century, the area attracted legions of rootless individuals. By 1890, it was estimated that over 9,000 homeless men, many with addictions, found lodging on the Bowery.

A variety of accommodations was available at a range of prices, from top-of-the-line rooms with clean sheets for 25 cents, to squalid hammocks in a windowless sub-basement for seven cents, or, for five cents, a mere flop spot on a wooden floor. Because of the harsh conditions exposed by reformer Jacob Riis, these lodging houses became regulated by law beginning in 1867. In 1909, local business owners saw the opening of the Manhattan bridge as a catalyst for change and an opportunity to improve the neighborhoods’ overall character. Local businessmen and realtors formed the Manhattan Bridge Transit Improvement Association in March 1916, seeking to get rid of the “cheap” lodging houses and entertainment show places, going as far as proposing to rename the Bowery Central Broadway. However, the Bowery’s reputation as a notorious “skid row” persisted into the 20th century. The elevated railway line, reconstructed in 1916, helped deter the redevelopment of this area for decades.

In 1961, a change in occupancy laws legalized the occupation of loft buildings, which led to the influx of many artists and creatives to the Bowery, and the development of the neighborhood’s character. In the 1950s, the GI bill and various other social programs were passed, clearing out many of the flop houses. an influx of Chinese immigrants in the late 50s and early 60s changed the area’s demographics, and expanded Chinatown northward along the Bowery. Homelessness, addiction, and vagrancy continued unabated well into the 1980s. Today, the Bowery is experiencing another period of change as new development, high-end boutiques, specialty shops, and restaurants replace the coexisting poets, artists, musicians, cultural and religious organizations and the legions of homeless men.

The Bowery Mission

227 Bowery was designed by William Jose and built in 1876 by owner Jonas Stolts, an undertaker and manufacturer of coffins. The Neo-Grec style building was altered in 1908-09 by architects Marshall L. Emery and Henry G. Emery when the Bowery mission leased the building. They added four stained glass windows (depicting the return of the prodigal son and attributed to Benjamin Sellers), and a small shed roof. The second floor interior was remodeled into a Gothic Revival style chapel.

The Bowery Mission is a religious-based organization established n 1879 by the Reverend and Mrs. Albert Gleason Ruliffson to “rescue and reform indigent homeless men.” In the beginning, the mission offered prayer services in a small room located at 14 Bowery, but as the services expanded, larger quarters were required and the mission utilized space at 36 Bowery (1880-1887) and 105 Bowery(1887-1898) until a massive fire and the death of the super-

intendent and founding member Mr. J Ward Childs put the mission in a crisis to remain open. Philanthropist and owner of Christian Herald, Dr. Louis Klopsch, purchased the Bowery Mission in 1895, saving it from closure and finally relocating it to it’s permanent home at 227 Bowery after construction was completed in 1909. Another important contributor was Sarah J. Bird, who conducted Thursday and Sunday prayer services from 1881-1914 without the aid of additional staff or a backing organization even before Klopsch’s purchase.

In 1895, the Mission added food service to it’s outreach, offering hearty meals for a nominal sum. In 1900, the mission came under the supervision of John Greener Hallimond who introuduced many innovative programs including an employment agency which helped over 1,000 men find employment per year outside of the city, even providing free transportation up to 50 miles away. He also founded the Bethesda Home in Brooklyn which helped young women find shelter and counseling. The Mission breadline began in 1902 to serve breafast to the homeless at 1:00am every morning between Thanksgiving and April 1st.

In 1894, with funds left over from a winter food drive the Bowery Mission established a Fresh Air Home in Nyack, NY for inner city children in need called the Mont Lawn Camp. During the Great Depression, the Bowery Mission expanded its services to the homeless and extended the hours of operation for their bread line, in part with help from the contribution of J.C. Penney in 1930.

​

In 1961 the Mont Lawn Camp was moved to Pennsylvania due to increasing sub urbanization. The doors of the Bowery Mission were painted their iconic red color by Frank Grande in 1973, in homage to his life transformation and the help of the Bowery Mission.The Mission didn’t expand it’s services to women until 1990, though it partnered with the Heartsease Inc. to open the Bowery Mission Women’s Center in 2005. In 2002, the Bowery Mission was renovated and free medical services were added for clients and the community.

Throughout the 2000s, the Bowery mission established residential programs for adults on the Upper east side, Harlem and East Harlem. They provided aid and refuge for those in need following the 9/11 attacks and Hurricane Sandy, and even remained open during the COVID-19 pandemic. The organization has continued to provide food, shelter, employment, and medical assistance to indigent homeless individuals for 147 years.

Since the 1870s, The Bowery Mission has been meeting essential needs and creating transformative community, helping people in New York City overcome homelessness and poverty through the love of Jesus.
