Friends Meeting House

137-16 Northern Boulevard

Built in 1694 by John Bowne and other Early Quakers, the Old Friends Meeting House is the oldest known and remaining religious house of worship in New York State, and the second oldest Quaker meeting house in the Nation. It is a prime example of Medieval architecture, and played a central role in the struggle for religious freedom in America. It stands today as a reminder of New York City’s earliest years, and honors the important contributions to the city made by the Society of Friends.

Friends Meeting House

The Friends Meeting House is the oldest remaining house of worship in New York State, and the first religious landmark designated by the LPC. The house is designated not only for it’s longevity and architecture, but also for it’s history as a place of worship and meeting, and it’s contributions to the freedom of religion in America.

Quaker History

Due to religious persecution faced in England, many Quakers or Friends sought refuge and settlement in the New World. In 1645, the town of Flushing, then called Vlissengen, was given a charter by the Dutch West India Company and became a part of New Netherlands. It was settled by large English families, who brought religious teachings which were met largely with acceptance throughout the town, despite governmental opposition. Peter Stuyvesant, at the time governor of New Netherlands, issued an edict forbidding colonists from entertaining a Quaker or hosting Quaker meetings in their house under penalty of a fifty pound fine.

After a respected colonist, Henry Townsend, was fined and banished for hosting a Quaker meeting in his home, Flushing citizens protested. They signed a document called the Flushing Remon-strance; one of the earliest demands for religious freedom made by American colonists. Despite this, the Quakers continued to face persecution at the hands of Stuyvesant.

In 1661, John Bowne bought the land which currently holds the Friends Meeting House, upon which he built a residence called the Bowne house. Knowing the risk he took, John invited the Friends to meet in his home, and later refused to pay the fine he incurred, for which he was banished from the colony. Bowne brought the case to Holland where he presented it to the Dutch West India Company. As a result, the "liberty of Conscience, according to the Custome and Manner of Holland," granted to Flushing in its charter of 1645, was extended in 1663 to establish religious liberty throughout the Province of New Netherlands.

After the English conquest, the Quakers of New York were given permission to hold meetings for worship, and they continued to meet at Bowne’s house twice a week for 30 years, until the meeting house was built and the first recorded meeting was held in 1694. The New York Yearly Meeting was established in 1695, and the first New York Yearly Meeting assembled at Flushing in 1696.

18th Century to Present

During the 18th century, the meeting house was briefly occupied by the British army, having been seized when the Quakers refused to join the war effort, and subsequently used as a hospital, stable, and storage. The New York Yearly meeting was moved to Long Island during this time, where it has remained ever since. Flushing instead become a local monthly meeting, which it remains today.

Quakers began to voice opposition to slavery as early as 1688, and in 1716, Preacher John Farmer called for the abolition of slavery in a meeting at the Flushing Meeting House. He was followed by other Quakers who supported abolition including Willianm Burling, John Woolman, Matthew Franklin, and Elias Hicks. It is important to note that before this, many Quakers had owned slaves, up until the revolution, and that despite thier disproval of slavery, African-Americans were still not socially accepted into the Religious Society of Friends until around the 1960s.

The building stands largely in its original form, using wood framing with an unusually steep hip roof, almost as tall as the two stories below it, the style of which can be traced back to medieval Holland. The south front contains two of double doors, one for women and one for men, and the design inside is purposefully simplistic, allowing for quiet, unencumbered worship. The land also contains a burial ground, which was begun in 1667, though graves were unmarked until 1835. The building faces south, away from the street, and is seperated by a stone wall which used to be a fence. In this seclusion from the passing traffic, a feeling of inherent peace survives in perfect accord with the simple charm of the grey shingled building and the sense of a long continued history of quiet prayer and work for the community.
