436-442 East 149th Street

Designed by George Keister and developed by George M. Cohan, this Italian Renaissance Revival-style theater and commercial building featured Broadway acts in its early “Subway Circuit” years before hosting a succession of clubs that were central to the Bronx’s innovative and influential Latin Music scene in the 1960s and 1970s.

Bronx Opera House

The Bronx Opera House recalls the rich history of its neighborhood as a center of Latin music and dance between the 1940s and 70s. Built as a theater and restaurant in 1912, the building served as entertainment center for over 6 decades.

History of the Bronx

Prior to European settlement, the area known as the Bronx was occupied by the Reckgawawanc tribe. The first European settlement was established along the Harlem river in 1693. in 1874 and 1895, a series of towns and villages were annexed from the city of New York including Melrose, Kingsbridge, Pelham, Wakefield, and Westchester. These annexed areas became the borough of the Bronx, and shortly after, local residents, business owners, and associations began pushing for improvements in infrastructure and transportation.

Beginnig in the 1880s, the Bronx became home to thousands of first and second generation immigrants seeking to escape the crowded tenements of East Harlem and the Lower East Side. It became one of the fastest growing urban areas throughout the early 90s, with it’s population reaching over 1 million by 1930. By the turn of the 20th century, the neighborhoods had completely transformed, sporting stores, restaurants, and major shopping districts. As early as the 1910s, theaters began appearing around a hub at the intersection of Third avenue el and IRT Subway, which became known as the 42nd street and Broadway of the Bronx.

The Bronx Opera house was one of 9 theaters located in the “Subway circuit”, a prominent shopping district and hub, easily reached via a 30 minute commute from Times Square. It brought “direct from Broadway” productions to local residents, which performed off-season and were more affordable than Broadway prices. Built by architect George Keister between 1912 and 1913, the Italian Renaissance Revival style theater comprised a front lobby/ commercial space as

well as an auditorium seating 2,500 people, though the auditorium has since been demolished. for the first 21 years, the auditorium was owned by George M. Cohan and his partner Sam Harris, while the front-facing lobby served as a restaurant. In 1916 the second floor became a private dance hall seating over 290 people.

Cohan was a prominent figure in show business, working as an actor, composer, playwright, producer, and much more. By the end of his career, he had written over 40 plays, collaborated in writing 40 others, produced 150 musicals, and composed over 500 songs, including the famous “The Yankee Doodle Boy.” Cohan and Harris ended thier partnership in 1920, and the Cosmopolitan Opera association took over the lease in 1929. The building continued to present comedies, operas, vaudville and stage shows until the mid 1930s, when it intermittently served as a movie house up to the 1980’s. during this time, the front building served as a gathering space for the community.

Latin Music at the Bronx Opera House

New York’s Latin music scene originated with the arrival of Cuban and Puerto Rican immigrants in the 1910s and 20s settling in Brooklyn waterfront and East Harlem neighborhoods. Prominent artists including Victoria and Rafael Hernandez moved to New York City providing their community with musical spaces and slowly gaining mainstream approval. Dedicated Latin music venues began opening in Harlem throughout the 1930s, and the genre took off with the success of bands such as The Afro-Cubans and singer Miguelito Valdes. New York City’s dense and diverse environment contributed to the diversity of musical influences.

Between the 1940’s and 1970s, the South Bronx became one of the Country’s leading Latin music centers, playing a crucial role in the creation an marketing of salsa. following WWII, many Puerto-Ricans and other East Harlem residents were displaced due to urban renewal projects. As old patrons of Bronx theaters, venues, and stores moved towards the suburbs, these places were overtaken by new Latin communities. musical influences continued to combine with the popularity of pachanga music (Cuban and Dominican influence) and boogaloo (Cuban and African-American influence).

The Bronx opera house contributed greatly to the popularity of Pachanga music and dance as it became the home to La Caravana, a club promoting Latin dance in 1959, and provided many artists with a venue including Manolin Morel Campos, Joe Quijano, Johnny Pacheco, and Charlie Palmieri. Performances were very popular and often sold out, solidifying the Bronx Opera House as the “Home of Pachanga.

La Caravana closed in 1962 and reopened in 1963 as the Bronx Casino, maintaining it’s reputation in the Latin music and dance scene. Performers included Jose Quijano, Rey Roig, Johnny Pacheco, Ray Barretto, Tito Rodriguez, Machito, and Tito Puentes.

The Bronx Casino closed in 1973, and reopened two years later yet again as El Cerromar. Like the previous two clubs, El Cerromar served as a venue for Latin Music performers including Orquestra Guarare, El Ritmo Tropical de Cuba, Orquestra Sabor de Angel Canales and Hector Rivera’s “Conjuncto Llave.” It remained open until at least 1980.

It appears that the theatre of the Bronx Opera House was vacant even before the closure of El Cerromar. It was proposed for designation as an interior landmark by the Landmarks Preservation Comittee in 1978-79, however the building’s owner had defaulted, and the mortgagor opposed the designation. Subsequently, the Department of Housing Preservation and Development temporarily sealed the building at the request of the preservation advocacy groups organized under the name of “South Bronx Cultural Center Fund”. Local officials and community began to campaign to protect the building.

In 1981, New York City took over ownership of the Bronx Opera House, which remained vacant until it was auctioned in 1983, when It briefly served as a Pentecostal church until 2008. Between 2008-09 the building was converted to a hotel, demolishing the theater auditorium to construct a 3-story office building in its place. The front building and public face of the Opera House was sensitively restored , and the Opera House Hotel opened in 2013. The facade still stands today as a testament to its storied past. The building remains an important part of the community, serving as a reminder of the rich history and cultural heritage of the Bronx and its central place in Latin music history.
