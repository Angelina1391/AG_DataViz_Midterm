Old Croton Aquaduct Walk

2200 Aqueduct Avenue

The Old Croton Aqueduct Walk is a linear park located on top of a historic water system constructed between 1837 and 1842. Considered an engineering marvel and a nationally signi-ficant model of urban infrastructure, the Aqueduct served as a crucial source of water for New York City, and almost immediately after its opening, became a beloved open space for residents.

Old Croton Aqueduct Walk

The Old Croton Aqueduct was a long sought after and vital water source for the City of New York. The aqueduct was a life sustaining, gravity powered, engineering marvel that allowed the city to continue to develop at an explosive rate through the 19th century. After its construction, the walkway atop the aqueduct become a favored path for New Yorkers, and today the Aqueduct Walk remains a significant reminder of New York City’s 19th century water infrastructure, while continuing to serve as a highly unique and popular open space for the Bronx community.

Colonial Water

Prior to the arrival of Dutch settlers, the Indigenous People had ample water sources. Within 100 years of the Island’s “purchase”, clean water had been nearly eviscerated. The Dutch colony of New Amsterdam suffered from overcrowding and a lack of a public water system. After taking over the colony, The English attempted to construct a public water system via 8 street wells, however a lack of waste management led to the contamination of these waters, and the rapid spread of disease.

In the early 1700s, tea pumps were a popular source of water for wealthy citizens. Enslaved people retrieved the water for these pumps until a series of fires in 1741, which led to suspicion of slave rebellion, multiple executions, and a law restricting the retrieval of water. This further limited the dwindling water supply.

In the early 18th century, industries built along the collect pond continued to contribute to the water’s contamination. In 1774, the first proposal for a city waterworks including a reservoir and aqueduct was presented by Christopher Colles. The proposal was accepted, however the American Revolutionary war halted the construction of this aqueduct.

In 1795, a major outbreak of Yellow Fever exacerbated the search for a clean water source. The Bronx was chosen for it’s proximity to the city, and in 1798, Dr. Joseph Browne presented a plan for its design. Browne’s brother in Law, Aaron Burr, used his political connections to persuade the state to pursue this plan, and his company, The Manhattan Company, was given the sole charter to bring water to New York City. Though the company succeeded in bringing water via a reservoir and large street wells, the project was costly and remained inaccessible to many.

Planning the Croton Aqueduct

Further outbreaks of disease such as Cholera in 1832 and 1834, as well as major fires such as the great fire of 1835, made it clear that an abundant source of clean water must be formed. Focus shifted between the Bronx river and the Croton river, as conflicting reports from engineers argued the inadequacies of the other. The Croton river was ultimately deemed superior, and commissioners were appointed in 1833 to carry out the project of providing the city with clean water.

​

However, The common council continued to fight with city and state politicians over financing of the aqueduct. In 1835, New York City was given three weeks to vote on the aqueduct. the vote passed 17,330 to 5,963 in favor of the Croton aqueduct. That same year, Major Douglass was hired as chief engineer.

Westchester residents opposed the aqueduct as it would go through their privately-owned land without providing them any of the water it carried. They protested the work, delaying the project further.The owners fought for their land rights, passing the Convenient Passage Act, requiring passage for the residents across the aqueduct, which settled the issue and contributed to the walk-ability of the aqueduct later in its history.

However, by mid 1836, little progress had been made, and the design was still incomplete. The commission fired Douglass and hired John B. Jervis. He began work in 1837 with a design similar to Douglas, but with increased size and decreased masonry to improve cost and efficiency. The aqueduct was broken into four sections, spanning from the Croton river to a distributing reservoir at 42nd street

Constructing the Croton Aqueduct

The construction was continuously halted by economic depression, poor working conditions, worker’s strikes, and uprisings. The laborers were primarily Irish immigrants with little bargaining power, and inequity was severe, leaving them grossly underpaid. A large strike followed a wage cut in 1839, where workers gathered in protest at Union Square. However, growing anti-labor sentiments led to the ignorance of these workers demands. Little documentation remains today of these important laborers who worked to bring the first direct water source to New York City

The aqueduct had an average height of 8.5 feet and average width of 7.5 feet. The length was lined with 8-12 inches of brick, and set into rubble with a stone foundation laid onto concrete. The aqueduct relied solely on gravity, and cut through hillsides, rock-faces, uneven ground, and at points was covered with stone and earthen embankment walls to maintain it’s downward slope. a total of 114 culverts were

constructed under the conduit to avoid the aqueduct’s water interacting with other streams, roads, and flood zones. 6 waste weirs were added along the aqueduct’s length to allow excess water run off. 33 ventilators were also added at intervals, and NYC’s oldest bridge (and a separate landmark), the High Bridge, was constructed in 1848 to carry Croton water across the Harlem River. Upon it’s completion, the aqueduct spanned 41 miles, and carried 45 million gallons of water daily.

Water ran through the old Croton Aqueduct to the distributing resevoir on July 4, 1842, and became available to the public during a large celebration on October 14. The aqueduct vastly changed the city, making clean drinking water readily accessible, decreasing contamination and disease, and providing ample water to put out fires.

19th Century to Present

By the 1860’s demand for indoor plumbing, increasing population, and increased waste led to the need for an expansion of the water supply system. In 1870, city officials attempted to increase the capacity of the Old Croton Aqueduct to 75 million gallons per day, however a drought in 1880-81 indicated the need for an entirely new aqueduct. that plans for a new aqueduct arose. Construction of the New Croton Aqueduct began in 1885 and was completed in 1890. Despite this, the old Croton aqueduct continued supplying water to New York City until 1955.

Soon after its completion, the linear embankment of the aqueduct became an important path for the residents of Fordham and University Heights. The Aqueduct Walk was closely tied to its surrounding community and residents sought to protect the open space it historically provided. One of the earliest frequenters of the walk was Edgar Allan Poe. As the Bronx transitioned into a developing neighborhood, many city planners, developers, and recreationalists proposed ideas for the aqueduct walk. In 1903, the city passed a trolley line along Aqueduct Avenue, which was promptly resisted by locals. The walk was threatened again in 1929 when the city sought to sell some of the land to developers.

former registrar of Bronx County, Edward Polak, wrote an open letter to the city in which he emphasized the use of this walk by thousands of pedestrians as a public park. In 1930, the Department of Water Supply and Electricity transferred the surface rights of the land to New York City’s Parks Department. In 1938, the Park’s department consulted architect Gilmore D. Clarke to design the new Aqueduct Walk. The walk today utilizes Clarke’s landscape plan, stone walls, and iron fencing. The park was officially opened in 1940 and has been utilized ever since.
