About The Project

Invisible Histories is an exploration of the stories behind a selection of New York City landmarks

At its most simple, a landmark is a building, monument, or location of special architectural, cultural, or historical importance that is often officially protected or preserved.

“The Landmarks Preservation Commission (LPC) is the largest municipal preservation agency in the nation. It is responsible for protecting New York City's architecturally, historically, and culturally significant buildings and sites by granting them landmark or historic district status, and regulating them after designation.”

​

Created in 1965, the Landmarks Preservation Commission (LPC) is a charter-mandated commission, dedicated to preserving and protecting buildings and sites with significance throughout New York City. The Commission was organized by Mayor Robert F. Wagner and aided by the signing of the Landmark Law after the devastating losses of historically significant buildings in the city, the most notable of which being Pennsylvania Station.

Designed by McKim, Mead and White and completed in 1911, this Beaux-Arts style architectural masterpiece occupied two entire blocks from 31st to 33rd streets between Seventh and Eighth avenues. Over 500 buildings were demolished for it’s initial construction. It’s central waiting room held the title as the largest indoor space in the city. Due to New Yorkers’ growing reliance on trains, Penn station was extremely popular, with ridership peaking in 1945 at 100 million passengers. However, following WWII ridership plummeted. Faced with a dilapidated building and fearing further financial losses, In 1961 the terminal’s leadership made a deal: to demolish the station to make room for a new entertainment arena- Madison Square Garden, and to rebuilt the terminal entirely underground.

This decision was heavily opposed with a group of architects calling themselves the Action Group for Better Architecture in New York (AGBANY) organizing a public protest against the demolition in 1962. The battle continued over the following year, gaining support from other organizations and city residents. However, these efforts were not enough to save the building, and the building was demolished between 1963 and 1966.

The Landmark Law, signed in 1965, was a direct result of the demolition and the emotional reactions to its destruction, creating an agency with governmental power to designate and preserve significant historic sites. This sparked a growing countrywide preservation movement and the National Historic Preservation Act was created shortly after.

The Landmark Law, states the purpose of safeguarding buildings and places representing New York City’s cultural, social. economic, political, and architectural history as:

  •  Stabilizing and improving property values
  •  Fostering civic pride
  •  Protecting and enhancing the City's attractions to tourists
  •  Strengthening the economy of the City
  •  Promoting the use of historic districts, landmarks, interior landmarks, and scenic landmarks for the education, pleasure and welfare of the people of the City

This project draws on the New York City Landmarks Preservation Commission’s public records of individual landmarks and historic districts, acessed through NYC Open Data. The dataset contains information on the designated areas including thier name, address, location, borough, creation date, designation date, architect, historic owner, building style, building type, alterations, and historic details. The data provides important insights into the vast range of places recognized as historically significant.

This project focuses on a few of the landmarks from this larger collection, which tell varied and vivid stories about New York City’s history, the significant people, places, and events tied to them. These landmarks do not solely represent New York City, rather it is the collection of specific histories told through places and the people who protect them which dictate what we remember and chose to preserve.

The geographic data has been used to create the interactive map. Information from the dataset appears directly in each landmark’s description, and the descriptions of the landmarks are based off of the LPC’s public designation reports. Additional research, historical documentation, photographs, and archival materials help expand upon the information contained in the original records. Many of the photos of the landmarks come from the LPC Designation Photo Archive, as well as the NYC Records and Information Service’s Municipal Archive.
