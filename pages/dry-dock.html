Dock Street at the foot of Third Street

Dry Dock Number 1 at the Brooklyn Navy Yard is a feat of engineering which persisted despite it’s turbulent and difficult creation. Utilized by the US Navy to repair and construct ships from1851-1996

this Dock was the first permanent Dry Dock in the New York area, and the third naval Dry Dock in the United States. It was a monumental project taking 10 years to complete and costing over 2 million dollars to create. The dock is significant for it’s contributions to the New York shipbuilding industry and history, and it continues to be utilized today.

Dry Dock No 1. Brooklyn Navy Yard

Dry docks are chambers below water level used for the repair and construction of ships. They function by filling with water, allowing a ship to enter from an adjoining body of water. the water is then drained from the dock, allowing the ship to rest on wooden blocks at the bottom so repairs can be made before the chamber is re-flooded and the ship returned to the water.

Planning for Construction

Plans for the Dock were first proposed in 1826 when the US Navy decided to build 3 Dry docks located in Boston, New York, and Virginia. However, a lack of funding delayed the construction of the New York Dock until the other two had been completed in 1836. The area was surveyed in 1837-38 and excav-ation began in 1841 under the direction of Edward H. Courtenay, a professor of civil engineering at West Point. However, funds ran out the following year and progress was halted yet again for an additional 2 years.

There was also considerable debate about the Dock’s location. The opening of the Old Croton Aqueduct in 1842 would have made Manhattan a more desirable location, where water from the aqueduct could be easily used to fill the dock. However, since no suitable site could be found on Manhattan, the decision was made to keep the dock in Brooklyn. Excavation resumed in 1844 with General William Gibbs McNiell as chief engineer. He was quickly suceeded in 1845 by W.P.S. Sanger, and him in 1846 by William J. McAlpine. Born and raised n New York, McAlpine was a major figure in engineering, having been

an apprentice to John B. Jervis from 1827-1836. During this time he gained valuable experience in con-structing railroads and canals. The Dry Dock was his first major project.

Construction Problems

The Dock’s construction was met by many obstacles, the first of which being the cofferdam, built across the mouth of the dry dock to keep water out during excavation. The original dam consisted of wood piles thirty feet long placed ten feet apart and was attached with planks and iron bolts and filled in with silt. This dam proved inadequate at keeping the water out, and a second dam, similar to the first was built inside of the first one. However, on July 3, 1846, both of the dams were breached, flooding the excavation site and delaying the project for 44 days. after a second breach in September, the decision was made

to construct an additional dam outside of the first two, which was made of piles 40 feet long, 15 inches square, and filled with gravel. This dam held much better than the first two.

Another issue faced was the existence of underground springs which constantly flooded the excavation site. These springs tended to spew out sand, undermining the structure’s foundations. McAlpine described in his reports how he dealt with these springs; First hollow pipes were put near the spring site, so that water could travel out, but the sand would remain in place. However, the water did not follow the pipes and instead flowed around them. Next, McAlpine attempted to fill the spring using 150 cubic feet of cobblestone and pebbles. These sank ten feet below the excavation level, and 50 more cubic feet were added, which ultimately failed to stop the water from flowing around them too. He then tried laying 6 feet of concrete with tubes to let the water out, which worked temporarily, until the spring broke through at another point 14 feet away. Finally, an area of one thousand square feet around the spring was planked, with dry cement, brick, and roman mortar laid atop. The stone foundations of the dry dock were successfully built upon this layer with small vent holes to allow the flow of water through the masonry.

as excavation was completed, tests of the area indicated quick sand spanning 75 feet deep. 6,539 piles were driven into the sand, alongside 1,744 tongue and groove sheet piles. the area between these piles was filled with concrete masonry to form a pile cap, and the top of the piles were leveled, covered with yellow pine timber, and layered with concrete. The structure utilizes about 23 thousand cubic yards of granite, sourced from mills in Maine and Connecticut. the stone near the bottom of the dock is laid in an inverted arch to withstand the uplifting forces. The dock is 30 feet wide and made of blocks roughly 2 feet wide and three feet deep. the sides of the dock are steeped, and the steps alternate between headers and stretchers.

​

In 1849, McAlpine was fired and replaced by Charles B. Stuart. soon after this, the cofferdams were removed and the first ship, the Dale, entered for repairs on January 8, 1850. work was completed by 1851, ten years after it was begun, and the dock was turned over to the commandment of the Navy Yard. At final count, the dock had cost approximately 2,146,000 dollars.

History of the Brooklyn Navy Yard

The Navy Yard became a major ship builder and outfitter during the civil war, building 16 warships, and converting an additional 416 commercial ships to warships during this time. The shipyard also launched a series of battleships during WWI. Dry dock number 1 was the only Dry Dock in use at the Brooklyn Navy Yard until WWII, when an additional 2 dry docks were built. During this time, the Brooklyn navy yard repaired 5,000 American and allied ships, and converted 250 to warships. Dry Dock number one continued to function and be utilized by the Navy until the shipyard closed in 1966, and was sold to the City of New York to build an industrial park. Despite this, Dry Dock number one continuies to be utilized in ship repair at the Brooklyn Navy Yard today.
