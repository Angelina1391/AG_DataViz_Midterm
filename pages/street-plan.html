Lower Manhattan, south of Wall Street

The street plan of lower Manhattan is a striking reminder of New York's colonial past and provides the only above-ground evidence of Dutch presence in New York during the 17th century. Evolving from Indian heritage, lower Manhattan became a governmental seat with the founding of Dutch "Nieuw Amsterdam" (1624-26) and, as New York, the English colonial capital after 1664. Lower Manhattan continues to evoke its colonial past through the configuration of its streets

Street Plan of New Amsterdam and Colonial New York

The Street Plan of New Amsterdam and Colonial New York is the last visible, physically obvious remnant of a major colonial city. The picturesque street plan with narrow curving roadways has accommodated the change and growth of a city since the Dutch settlement in the 1600s.

New Amsterdam History

The Dutch first began settling in New York with Henry Hudson’s voyage of the Hudson River in 1609. By this time, they had slowly established a fur trade with the Indigenous Peoples, though their population was rapidly declining due to disease and relocation. Dutch families created a settlement on Nut Island ( today Governor's Island) in 1624, and in 1626, Peter Minuit “purchased” the island of Manhattan from the Algonquin People. The dutch colony of Nieuw Amsterdam began to take shape under the guidance of Crijn Frederickz, who was appointed to designed the layout of the city by the Dutch West India Company.

​

New Amsterdam prioritized defense against the Indigenous People and the invading English. Fredericksz was instructed to construct a four bastioned fort on the site of what is today the US Custom House, with streets which followed the land’s natural geography. The Indigenous People’s Wickquasgeck trail was converted to a road called Broadway, and an existing inlet was transformed into a canal (current day Broad Street). New Amsterdam’s burgomasters attempted to regulate the town’s growth, keeping buildings close together to maximize defense .In the early years of settlement, these burgomasters also oversaw the planning, opening, closing, grading, and widening of city thoroughfares and alleys.

The fur trade with Indigenous Peoples was the primary means of New Amsterdam’s economy. However, the Dutch hoped to expand trade with the Iroquis People who lived west of Dutch settlements in current day Albany. In 1643, without provocation, the Dutch massacred dozens of Algoquin families, launching a series of wars between the Colonists and the Indigenous People. The wars drained New Amsterdam’s economy, and did not end until the English arrival in 1664, where Peter Stuyvesant surrendered the colony to the British.

The Dutch colony of New Amsterdam consisted of about 2,240 people by 1664, who were largely Polish or Irish catholic men. Surprisingly, the majority of the New Amsterdam population was not Dutch. Throughout the colonial era, between 7 and 20% of the population was black, both enslaved and free. 23 male Jewish refugees from Brazil arrived in 1654. Despite this expanding and diverse population, the original city plan was able to accommodate and shift to fit the residents’ need.

Multiple surveys of New Amsterdam were performed by Frederick de Koningh and Jaques Cortelyou which produced detailed renderings of the existing city in 1660. the original no longer exists, but a redrawn version called the Castello Plan remains. It is accomponied by a list of people and houses made by Nicasius de Sille. These are the oldest surviving reliable accounts of New Amsterdam’s street patterns.

British Colonial History

After the English took power in 1664, subsequent surveys show that the roads remained in relatively the same configuration., with the addition of a few streets, and the extension of others. multiple colonial city plans were created throughout the 18th century, including the William Bradford map in 1730 created by James Lyne. This map showed the city’s growth, as defined by the expansion of city boundaries and the addition af Montgomerie’s Ward. The Montresor plan in 1765-66, and the Bernard Ratzer plans in 1766-67 show the colonial city at it’s height preceding the American Revolutionary War. Contemporary cartographers have redrawn the maps to reconstruct the colonial city, and were published in 1976 as the Atlas of Early American History.

The maps clearly show how the street patterns were determined by and evolved around their natural environment, rather than rigidly-imposed patterns or grids. Additionally, the layout reflected the Colonial economy and defense, following existing paths and prioritizing access to the rivers for trade. This is shown through the attention paid to the extensive dock areas. This organic growth was practical and economical as it meant terrain did not have to be leveled and settlement could occur faster. The natural terrain only changed when it became economically necessary, near the waterside where commerce was kept close to the town’s economic base. Large manufacturing spaces were not yet needed, and even after the English conquest in 1664, manufacturing beyond local consumption was forbidden by law. Unlike other towns, religion did not play a role in city planning, and though religious observance was important, it had no effect on the layout of the town. This in turn made the colony a popular place for diverse settlers with varying religious backgrounds.

The Streets Involved

Beaver Street extending from Broadway to Pearl Street. The name refers to the Dutch fur trade which contributed to the colony’s wealth

​

Bridge Street extending from Whitehall Street to Broad Street. Marks the location of one of three bridges constructed by the Dutch to cross the canal at Broad Street.

Broad Street extending from Wall Street to Pearl Street. Originated as an inlet running from the east river to Exchange. The Dutch enlarged the inlet into a Canal and extended streets on either side. the canal was filled and the street paved in 1676 resulting in today’s Broad Street.

​

Broadway extending from Wall Street to Stone Street. Originally the Wickquasgeck trail used by Indigenous People meaning birch-bark country. route led through Bronx and and Westchester to north of present-day Albany. Oldest thoroughfare in New York City and one of the oldest in North America.

​

Exchange Place extending from Broadway to Hanover Street. Named in 1827 after the construction of the Merchant’s exchange building at wall and William, the predecessor of the Citibank building (it’s own landmark).

​

Hanover Square at the intersection of Stone Street and Pearl Street. Dates back to creation in 1730, originally located on the Manhattan shoreline.

Hanover Street extending from Wall Street to Pearl Street.

Marketfield Street extending from New Street to Broad Street. Designates the location of the first Dutch livestock market in New Amsterdam. Later known during English colonization as Petticoate lane, as prostitutes often gathered at the west end near the fort.

​

Mill Lane extending from South William Street to Stone Street. Originally ran from a mill built in 1628 to grind bark used by tanners.

New Street extending from Wall Street to Marketfield Street. First street added by the British to the Dutch settlement after gaining control of the colony.

Pearl Street extending from Whitehall Street to Wall Street. formed the original shoreline of the East River. Also assumes the idea that pearls, supposedly found in American waters, would bring a fortune.

​

South Williams Street extending from Broad Street to William Street. earlier names point to it’s condition as a muddy lane, site of glazier Evert Duuckingh’s dwellings, multiple mills, and a Jewish synagogue.

​

Stone Street extending from Broadway to Broad Street. Became the first paved street in New York, surfaced with cobblestones at the request of its residents in 1655.

Wall Street extending from Broadway to Pearl Street. The Dutch had built a wall here sometime between 1639 and 1653, used to defend the city from attack from the North by Indigenous groups and the English.

​

Whitehall Street extending from Pearl Street to Stone Street. emerged with the construction of the Government House (US Customs House)

William Street extending from Wall Street to Hanover Square. Named for Willem Beeckham, a Dutch immigrant who served 9 terms as mayor in New Amsterdam.
