Located at the Southernmost point of Staten Island, this landmark is the city’s first to recognize the thousands of years of Indigenous People’s habitation. The site includes approximately 20 acres of highly sensitive archaeological land. It’s designation also functions to protect the below-ground archaeological resources from further excavation.

Archaic period

Indigenous presence in this area dates back to approximately 8,000 years ago, a period called the Early Archaic. During this time, the site would have laid more inland, being heavily forested by pine and oak trees and surrounded by swamp and marsh from glacial melt. Objects found dating back to this period include wood working, basket making, and hide rendering tools. A series of hearths were rediscovered in 1960, which served as the center for the Indigenous population’s domestic activities including butchering, cooking, and tool-making.

During the Middle Archaic Period, 7,000- 5,000 years before present, the ecology shifted to include more deciduous trees such as oak, chestnut, and hemlock. The Indigenous People’s diet also changed to include marine resources. They practiced coastal fishing, and shellfish gathering. Stone projectile points used for hunting game were also found.

During the Late Archaic period, about 4,000-3,000 years ago, the forest shifted once again, now growing oak and chestnut trees, which produced nuts and acorns. These became a staple part of the diets of Indigenous People (called “mast”) as well as the native deer, bear, raccoon, and turkey. Technological innovations occurred and continued into the Transitional or Terminal Archaic period, where habitats stabilized and cultural traditions among the people in this region formed.

Spanning 2,500 years, the Woodland Period was marked by changes in settlement patterns and the adoption of small-scale farming. Agriculture included maize, beans, and squash,

but Indigenous people still relied on marine foods, including the extensive beds of oysters in the surrounding waters. evidence of ritual and social smoking, fragments of pottery, and food scraps were found. During this period, there is strong evidence the area was inhabited by Lenape, Munsee speaking people. Some artifacts, like Olivella shells which are not native to the area indicate contact with other Munsee people.

European Exploration and colonization

The Woodland period ended with the first settlement of Europeans in the early 1600’s. At this time, Staten Island was inhabited by the Raritan people, who gave the island it’s name (Aakawaxung Munahanung). Tangible evidence of interactions between Indigenous People and Europeans was found at the site incluing traded European iron, copper, and brass. Growing trade with the Eueopeans also led to disruptions in local economy, war, disease, and dislocation.

​

The arrival of Henry Hudson in 1609, and the start of permanent European settlements marked the beginning of violent encounters between the two groups, including two wars and major forced relocation. Historical land deeds found from this time indicate the Indigenous People had rightfully given land to the Europeans. However scholarly interpretation of these documents suggest that the Indigenous participants likely viewed them more as easements or tenancies than a sale. This lead to many more years of conflict. The remainder of Staten Island was ‘”sold” to the British in 1670.

During this time, the majority of Staten island belonged to Englishman Captain Christopher Billopp. In 1676, Billopp constructed a 2.5 story feildstone residence known as the Billopp House, or later the Conference house (this was designated by the LPC as a separate landmark in 1967.) There is little evidence of Indigenous People’s presence in this area after Billopp’s acquisition, though family accounts claim Indigenous groups continued to live in Staten Island into the 20th century. After Billopp’s death in 1725, the Island served as a strategic location during the revolutionary war. During this time, the manor was owned by Billopp’s great grandson, Colonel Christopher Billopp,

19th Century to Present

The landmark site was used by the Ward family for farming until 1835. In 1858, excavation of the Cole-Decker house constructed on the landmark site revealed archaeological resources, leading to a variety of professional archaeologists and non-professional collectors to search for artifacts over the next century. Features found included middens, or piles of discarded shells, food waste, tools, and sometimes culturally sensitive materials. Shell piles can be seen at the surface today, which may indicate middens beneath. The area slowly developed over time. in the late 19th and early 20th century, local historians and politicians began advocating for the protection of the conference house, until the property was donated to the City of New York in 1926. In 1937, the site was opened to the public as a recreational park.

It is important to note that much of the archaeological work performed at this site occurred before modern archaeological standards and ethics were set. This work was performed without any input from the Tribal Nations. The LPC’s research and designation of this site involved collaborative engagement with the city’s federally recognized tribes, who provided meaningful input and guidance regarding resources, preferred terminology, and the landmark name. The designation now serves to protect this site, and is the city’s first to recognize and honor the long and significant presence of Indigenous Peoples in New York City.
